\chapter{Lateral Movement and Multi-System Correlation}
\label{cap:correlacion_multisistema}

An intrusion rarely remains confined to a single host when systems share local subnets, cross-mounted NFS exports, or inter-user trust relationships. Forensic correlation across peer departmental servers enabled the reconstruction of lateral movement paths and substantiated hypotheses regarding session origins.

\begin{notaforense}[The Lateral Movement Vector (\textit{Pivoting})]
Pivoting consists of leveraging a compromised intermediary host as a jump box to stealthily probe, attack, or administer other servers situated on the same internal network segment. By hopping from \texttt{atlas} into \texttt{helios}, the adversary achieved two distinct operational advantages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perimeter Firewall Evasion:} Inter-system connections between \texttt{atlas} and \texttt{helios} originated from the trusted internal subnet (\texttt{192.0.2.0/24}), bypassing any perimeter access control lists restricting external inbound SSH or Telnet.
    \item \textbf{Origin Obfuscation:} Within \texttt{helios}'s direct connection logs, the apparent source IP address was that of \texttt{atlas} (\texttt{192.0.2.20}), compelling examiners to expand the forensic perimeter into peer server logs to unmask the true public ISP pool address.
\end{enumerate}
\end{notaforense}

Logins for accounts \texttt{zopilote}, \texttt{zena}, \texttt{proa}, and \texttt{lllll} originated from residential DSL endpoints assigned to Prodigy, Telemedia, and Terra, all geo-located within the city of Morelia. Noticeably, when access to several compromised student accounts was revoked, active sessions for user \texttt{duende} immediately surfaced.

The inbound sessions for user \texttt{duende} leaping from \texttt{atlas} to \texttt{helios} are confirmed in \texttt{atlas}'s accounting records, proving that they originated from external Prodigy Infinitum DSL subscriptions.

With lateral pivoting through \texttt{atlas} documented and tied to residential internet providers in Morelia, a grave investigative question remained: what of the institutional mail server (\texttt{io.ifm.lab.edu.mx})? If the adversary harvested passwords through the trojanized sniffer on \texttt{helios}, had they penetrated private institutional mailboxes? Did they leverage the webmail portal (\textit{OpenWebMail}) to read communications or impersonate departmental personnel? To resolve the operational extent of this threat, the forensic team audited MTA transport records and CGI session logs on \texttt{io}, detailed in Chapter~\ref{cap:servidor_correo_webmail}.

\begin{retoforense}[Multi-Hop Pivoting Traceability]
Using the correlated log excerpts from \texttt{atlas} and \texttt{helios}, reconstruct the end-to-end connection chain: what was the originating external dial-up/DSL ISP IP address, and across what ports were successive sessions established? What subscriber records would be requested from the telecommunications carrier under a legal subpoena to tie the session to a billing subscriber?
\end{retoforense}

\begin{perspectiva20}[Contemporary Forensic Perspective: Zero Trust and Microsegmentation]
The traditional perimeter security model of 2004 assumed that any internal network traffic was inherently trustworthy, enabling a compromised host like \texttt{atlas} to target peer systems without friction. Two decades later, the dominant architectural paradigm is \textit{Zero Trust} ("never trust, always verify"): internal campus networks are microsegmented to block unauthorized east-west lateral movement, and every session must be authenticated cryptographically via mTLS and continuously assessed against dynamic risk policies.
\end{perspectiva20}
